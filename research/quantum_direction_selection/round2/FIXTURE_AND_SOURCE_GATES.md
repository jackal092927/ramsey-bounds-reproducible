## 1. Sixteen-vertex independent-filling fixture

**Status: PROVABLE AS STATED.** This verifies the elementary topology used in the quotient construction, not the King--Kohler gadget spectrum or a quantum runtime advantage.

Let $F$ be two four-edge circles joined at a single vertex: seven vertices and eight edges. It is a flag graph with $H_1(F;\mathbb R)=\mathbb R^2$. Take disjoint copies $F_A,F_B$ and their simplicial join $R=F_A*F_B$. Fix one circle $C_A\subset F_A$ and $C_B\subset F_B$. Form

$$Y_A=(F_A\cup\operatorname{cone}C_A)*F_B,
\qquad Y_B=F_A*(F_B\cup\operatorname{cone}C_B),$$

and the filtration $X_0=R\subset X_1=Y_A\subset X_2=Y_A\cup Y_B$. There is no edge between the two new cone vertices. Future vertices may be isolated at earlier levels. All three are clique complexes of graphs on the same sixteen vertices.

**Claim.** In degree three,

$$\beta_3(X_0)=4,\quad\beta_3(X_1)=2,\quad\beta_3(X_2)=1,$$

and both induced maps are surjective. Their successive persistence ratios are $1/2$ and $1/2$.

**Proof.**

1. The reduced join Kunneth formula over a field gives $H_3(R)=H_1(F_A)\otimes H_1(F_B)$ of dimension four. Write the bases as $a,a'$ and $b,b'$, with $a,b$ the chosen circles.
2. Relative homology of adding the first cone, joined with $F_B$, is concentrated in degree four, with dimension two. Its connecting homomorphism has image $\operatorname{span}\{a\otimes b,a\otimes b'\}$. The long exact sequence therefore identifies $H_3(X_1)$ with the quotient by that image and makes the inclusion surjective.
3. The second gadget intersects the first only in $R$. Excision identifies its relative attachment with $(Y_B,R)$, again with relative homology in degree four and none in degree three. The connecting map adds the classes $a\otimes b,a'\otimes b$. The first is already zero after Step 2, and the second is independent. Thus $H_3(X_2)$ is the one-dimensional quotient generated by $a'\otimes b'$, and the second map is surjective. Extra degree-four homology is immaterial to this degree-three claim.
4. Assign weight one to register vertices and any common positive weight $\lambda$ to cone vertices. The weighted boundary is conjugate to the ordinary boundary by the diagonal matrices with entries $\prod_{v\in\sigma}w(v)$. These conjugations commute with the filtration inclusions. Hence all counts and induced ranks remain unchanged. This statement does not imply a weight-independent spectral gap. $\square$

The reproducible numerical check is `check_kernel_filtration.py`, using NumPy/SciPy versions already recorded in the parent README. On this machine it finished in under one second. For $\lambda\in\{1,1/2,1/4,1/8\}$ all Betti numbers and ranks matched. It checks $\partial_3\partial_4=0$, computes harmonic kernels, and independently computes persistence by concatenated cycle/boundary ranks. Degree-three chain sizes are $64,124,184$. At $\lambda=1/8$, the two filled complexes' smallest positive eigenvalues were approximately $0.015625$. Numerical thresholds are explicit in the script; the exact topology above does not depend on them. No quantum state-vector or device experiment is claimed.

## 2. Source threshold gate: avoid an unjustified amplification inference

The [July normalized-persistence preprint, Definition 2 and Section 5.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.03278v1) allows arbitrary decision thresholds $a-b\geq1/\operatorname{poly}(n)$, while imposing perfect acceptance on a subspace $S$ and acceptance at most $r=1/3$ on $S^\perp$. It then amplifies rejection on $S^\perp$ and uses the normalized dimension of $S$ to decide the original instance. That last implication needs an additional threshold argument.

Let $A$ be its acceptance operator, $D$ the mixed-input dimension, $f=\dim S/D$, and $p=\operatorname{Tr}(A)/D$. Positivity and the stated spectral promise give exactly

$$f\leq p\leq r+(1-r)f.$$

Thus a YES instance with $p\geq a$ only guarantees $f\geq(a-r)/(1-r)$, whereas a NO instance with $p\leq b$ guarantees $f\leq b$. A sufficient separation condition is

$$a-r-(1-r)b\geq1/\operatorname{poly}(n).$$

The condition $a-b\geq1/\operatorname{poly}(n)$ alone does not imply it. For example the acceptance operators $A_{\rm yes}=I/4$ and $A_{\rm no}=0$ both have perfect-acceptance space zero and satisfy the off-space bound $1/3$, while $a=1/4,b=1/8$ separates their traces. Both exact kernel fractions remain zero under any amplification that preserves the perfect-acceptance space. These operators are realized by a fixed clean-qubit rotation or no rotation, ignoring the mixed input, in a fixed universal gate set augmented by that rotation. This is a counterexample to this scalar implication, not a proof that the whole source complexity claim admits no other reduction.

For the standard constants $a=2/3,b=1/3,r=1/3$, however,

$$f_{\rm yes}\geq1/2,\qquad f_{\rm no}\leq1/3.$$

The gap is $1/6$. Estimating $f$ to error $1/24$ and comparing against $5/12$ decides the promise directly; no acceptance amplification or exact-gate conversion is needed. This is the appropriate restricted source promise for our current transfer attempt.

## 3. Exact-gate and locality boundary

The July source explicitly states that its perfect-completeness class can depend on the fixed gate set. Approximate gate compilation does not preserve exact kernels and cannot be silently used here. A promising restriction is an exact rational real gate set such as $\{X,\mathrm{CX},\mathrm{CCX},H\otimes H\}$. Its constant-size history propagation constraints can be decomposed into a fixed finite palette of projectors onto integer vectors after clearing denominators. This observation still requires an explicit circuit-to-history convention and full supported-palette check before declaring the reduction complete.

Standard unary-clock propagation may use more qubits per term than the June source's special four-local construction. The normalized-persistence argument must use its actual fixed locality $m$ and exponent $4m+2$, not automatically reuse the June exponent eighteen. All parameter bounds stay polynomial for fixed $m$, but their exponents must be stated honestly.
